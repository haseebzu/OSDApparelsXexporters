# Apparel Sample Types: Proto, Fit and Pre-Production

A clothing sample is a decision point, not simply a miniature version of the finished order. Brands normally use different samples to answer different questions: whether the design can be made, whether it fits as intended, and whether the approved specification is ready for bulk production. Clear sample feedback helps keep the product brief, measurements, materials and decoration aligned before a larger order is confirmed.

## Start with one clear brief

Before asking for any sample, send the product type, measurements or tech pack, material direction, colour references, decoration placement, trim details, labels, packaging requirements, quantity and destination country. Reference images help explain the desired look, but they do not replace measurements, construction notes or artwork files.

A good brief also identifies which details are still open for discussion. For example, a brand may know the intended fit and print placement but need to confirm the appropriate fabric weight or construction. Marking those decisions clearly makes the review easier.

## Proto samples test the idea

A prototype, often called a proto sample, is used to turn an early product idea into something that can be reviewed. The focus is usually the overall concept: silhouette, construction direction, pockets, panels, neck shape, hood shape or other product features.

A prototype is useful when the specification is incomplete. It gives the buyer a concrete garment to discuss and can reveal questions that were not visible in a flat sketch. It should not be treated as final approval if fabric, measurements, artwork or trims are still under review.

## Fit samples test measurements and balance

A fit sample is reviewed against the intended size chart or measurement specification. The buyer should check the points that matter to the garment: body length, width, sleeve shape, neck opening, rise, inseam or other relevant construction details.

Feedback works best when it is specific. Instead of saying that a garment feels too large, note which measurement or area should change and whether the change applies across the size range. Keep the approved measurement table with the comments so the next version can be compared accurately.

## Pre-production samples record the approved direction

A pre-production sample is the final review stage before bulk production is confirmed. It should reflect the approved product direction, including the agreed construction, fabric, colour, decoration, labels, trims and packaging where relevant. The buyer should review it against the latest written specification rather than relying on memory or an earlier sample.

Use a written approval record. It should identify the sample version, date, product reference, measurements, colour reference, artwork revision and any remaining actions. If a change is required, state whether another sample or a revised approval is needed before production moves ahead.

## Review decoration and labels in context

Artwork can look different once it is applied to a garment. Check scale, placement, legibility, colour relationship and how the decoration sits on the selected fabric. For labels and packaging, confirm the legal and brand information the buyer needs for the destination market. OSD confirms the commercial details, including MOQ, price, sampling and lead time, in the quote.

## Questions to ask before approving

### Which details are final?

Confirm the measurements, fabric direction, colour reference, decoration placement, trims, labels and packaging. Flag anything still awaiting a decision.

### Who gives the final approval?

Name the buyer-side person responsible for consolidating comments. Multiple uncoordinated feedback sources can create conflicting instructions.

### What happens if a detail changes later?

Ask how the requested change affects the quotation and whether a further sample review is needed. Do not assume a change is included without written confirmation.
